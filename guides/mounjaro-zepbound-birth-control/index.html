GLP-1s & contraception

Mounjaro, Zepbound and the pill: the 4-week rule

The tirzepatide label has a specific instruction for people on oral birth control. Here's what it says, why it's there, how the windows add up during titration, and why semaglutide's label is different.

By the Revé editorial team · Updated Nov 18, 2026

Short answer

The US prescribing information for Mounjaro and Zepbound (tirzepatide) tells people who use oral hormonal birth control to switch to a non-oral method, or add a barrier method such as condoms, for 4 weeks after the first dose and for 4 weeks after every dose increase. The reason is that tirzepatide slows stomach emptying, which can reduce how much of a pill is absorbed, and the effect is strongest after a new dose.

IUDs, implants, patches, rings and injections are not expected to be affected. Semaglutide (Ozempic, Wegovy, Rybelsus) does not carry the same instruction.

What the label says

The Mounjaro and Zepbound prescribing information both carry the same instruction for people using oral hormonal contraceptives: switch to a non-oral method, or add a barrier method, for 4 weeks after starting tirzepatide and for 4 weeks after each dose escalation.

Three details people miss:

  • It covers pills only. That includes combined pills and progestin-only pills. Lilly's medical information says hormonal methods that are not taken by mouth, such as an IUD, implant, patch, ring or injection, should not be affected.
  • It resets with every increase. An increase is treated like a new start at that dose, so a new 4-week window opens.
  • It is about tirzepatide. Mounjaro is approved for type 2 diabetes and Zepbound for weight management, but they are the same molecule and carry the same advice.

Australia's regulator, the TGA, reviewed the question separately. It found that reduced pill effectiveness during starts and dose increases could not be ruled out, and added the same 4-week advice to the Australian product information as a precaution.

Why tirzepatide affects the pill

Tirzepatide slows how fast the stomach empties. Anything swallowed, including a contraceptive pill, reaches the intestine later and can be absorbed less completely. Lilly reports that this slowing is greatest after a single starting dose and fades with continued use at the same dose, which is why the windows follow starts and increases rather than lasting forever.

In the interaction study behind the label, women took one dose of tirzepatide alongside a combined pill (ethinyl estradiol with norgestimate). Peak hormone levels fell by more than half and total exposure fell by about a fifth, according to an NHS pharmacy summary of the data. Reviewers considered that large enough to matter for contraception.

How the windows add up during titration

The standard tirzepatide schedule raises the dose no sooner than every 4 weeks until a maintenance dose is reached. If each increase happens at the 4-week mark, the backup windows touch end to end:

PeriodWhat happensLabel backup window?
Weeks 1 to 4First doseYes, 4 weeks after starting
Weeks 5 to 8First increaseYes, 4 weeks after an increase
Weeks 9 to 12Second increaseYes, 4 weeks after an increase
Each later increaseAnother step upYes, 4 more weeks each time
4 weeks after the final increase, onwardSame dose continuesNo label instruction for backup

In practice: if your dose goes up every 4 weeks, the label's advice covers the entire titration, from the first injection until 4 weeks after the last increase. Your prescriber sets your actual schedule. This table only shows how the windows line up.

Ozempic, Wegovy and Rybelsus are different

Semaglutide, the active ingredient in Ozempic, Wegovy and Rybelsus, does not carry a 4-week backup instruction. Its product information reports that semaglutide did not change exposure to a combined pill (ethinyl estradiol with levonorgestrel) to a clinically relevant degree, and states that it is not expected to reduce pill effectiveness. A separate study of oral semaglutide found no effect on those hormone levels either.

That difference is the source of a lot of confusing advice online. The tirzepatide instruction is specific and label-based. Semaglutide's label does not include it.

What newer research says

A 2026 analysis presented to ob-gyns, reported by Healio, suggested GLP-1 and dual GIP/GLP-1 medications are not likely to reduce pill effectiveness overall. It found ethinyl estradiol exposure held steady across studies while some progestins were absorbed more slowly or less. The same researchers still advised contraception counseling when treatment starts and when the dose goes up. The tirzepatide label instruction has not changed, and it is the instruction your prescriber and pharmacist work from.

Vomiting, diarrhea and missed pills

Separate from the label windows, any medication that causes vomiting or severe diarrhea can stop a pill from being absorbed. Nausea and vomiting are most common in the days after starting or increasing a GLP-1 dose. If you vomit soon after taking a pill or have severe diarrhea, follow the missed-pill instructions in your pill's leaflet and ask a pharmacist.

Compounded tirzepatide

The FDA does not review compounded GLP-1 medications for safety, effectiveness or quality before they are sold, and compounded tirzepatide has no FDA label of its own. It is the same active ingredient, so the same effect on stomach emptying would be expected. The FDA has also reported dosing errors with compounded semaglutide and tirzepatide, so it is worth confirming exactly when your dose changed.

Keeping track of your windows

The windows are easy to lose track of because they hang on dates: the first injection and every increase. Write those dates down, then count 4 weeks forward from each one. A paper log works. In Revé, each dose you log keeps its date and your cycle day, so the date of every change is already in your history when you need to count forward or show your prescriber.

Questions

Questions about tirzepatide and birth control

Does Mounjaro make birth control pills less effective?

The label says it may. Tirzepatide slows stomach emptying, which can lower how much of an oral contraceptive is absorbed, and the effect is largest after the first dose and after each increase. That is why the label tells pill users to switch to a non-oral method or add a barrier method during those 4-week windows.

How long do I need backup contraception on Zepbound?

For 4 weeks after your first dose and for 4 weeks after every dose increase. If your prescriber raises the dose every 4 weeks, the windows run back to back, so backup covers the whole titration until 4 weeks after the final increase.

Does the 4-week rule apply to an IUD, implant, patch, ring or shot?

Lilly's medical information says hormonal contraceptives that are not taken by mouth should not be affected. The instruction is about pills.

Do Ozempic or Wegovy have the same warning?

No. Semaglutide's product information reports no clinically relevant change in exposure to a combined pill, and does not tell pill users to add backup. General advice still applies: vomiting or severe diarrhea can reduce absorption of any pill.

Does the rule apply to compounded tirzepatide?

Compounded tirzepatide is not FDA-approved and has no FDA label of its own. It is the same active ingredient, so the same stomach-emptying effect would be expected. Ask the prescriber who wrote it.

What if I lower my dose or restart after a break?

The label instruction covers starting and dose increases. It does not spell out restarts after a pause, so ask your prescriber or pharmacist before relying on the pill alone.
